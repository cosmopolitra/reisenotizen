# Jeju by Stages: A Route from Volcanic Heights to Southern Falls

![A winding coastal road crosses green hills beside rocky shoreline, with a large mountain rising in the distance under a partly cloudy sky.](https://forschungsergebnisse.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/1795342743944.webp)

Jeju’s highest mountain stands in the middle of the island. Volcanic cones rise around it, lava tunnels run beneath it, and waterfalls spill toward the southern coast. The independent guide to [Jeju Province](https://jeju-province.org/) helps visitors connect those sights into regional routes.

Start in the east with Seongsan Ilchulbong, a crater above the sea. Then explore the coast, meet the island’s diving tradition, and head south to Seogwipo, where Jeongbang Falls drops toward the shore.

Jeju rewards a slower route. Udo needs a ferry, Hallasan’s summit needs a full hiking day, and an eastern bus journey can involve several connections. Group nearby sights together and there is more time to see them, rather than watch them pass through a vehicle window.

## Hallasan: The Mountain at the Heart of Jeju

Hallasan rises 1,950 meters above sea level, making it South Korea’s highest mountain. Its broad volcanic slopes occupy the center of Jeju, with the crater lake Baengnokdam at the top.

The mountain is not Jeju’s only volcanic landmark. Roughly 360 smaller cones, known as *oreum*, dot the island. They give the land its uneven outline, from the mountain’s flanks to the coast.

In 2007, UNESCO inscribed three parts of the island as a World Heritage property: Hallasan, the Geomunoreum lava-tube system, and Seongsan Ilchulbong. Together, they show how volcanic activity built both Jeju’s peaks and its hidden passages.

The protected property covers 9,521.8 hectares, with a further 9,324.2 hectares in a buffer zone. The UNESCO page on [Jeju volcanic heritage](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1264/) explains the features that link the mountain, caves, and coastal crater.

## Choosing a Hallasan Trail

Two trails reach Baengnokdam: Seongpanak and Gwaneumsa. The climb is demanding, and the ascent alone takes about five hours. The descent still lies ahead, so this is a day for walking, not a quick stop between attractions.

Summit access uses a reservation system. The usual daily limits are 1,000 hikers on Seongpanak and 500 on Gwaneumsa. Use the official [Hallasan hiking reservations](https://visithalla.jeju.go.kr/contents/contents.do?id=49&language=en_US) page when arranging a summit trip.

Several other trails do not require reservations, including Eorimok, Yeongsil, Donnaeko, Eoseungsaengak, and Seokguram. Eorimok, Yeongsil, and Donnaeko do not connect to the summit, so choose Seongpanak or Gwaneumsa if Baengnokdam is the goal.

Bring food in reusable containers. Disposable lunch containers are prohibited in the national park. A picnic box that travels back down with you keeps both the meal and its packaging out of the mountain landscape.

## Seongsan Ilchulbong: Sunrise Peak Above the Sea

Seongsan Ilchulbong rises about 180 meters above sea level on Jeju’s eastern edge. Its wide crater sits above steep sides, with the sea surrounding much of the cone.

The crater is roughly 600 meters across and 90 meters deep. It was once farmed, but silver grass now covers its floor. Long before the fields, the cone stood apart from Jeju as an island. Sand and soil gradually joined it to the mainland.

Allow about 30 to 40 minutes to climb to the summit. From the top, look toward Udo, the island off the eastern coast. The view makes a useful introduction to a later ferry trip, even if Udo is not on that day’s schedule.

Published admission is ₩5,000 for adults and ₩2,500 for children and teenagers. Seasonal opening times range from 4:30 a.m. in summer to 6 a.m. in winter. Ticket sales stop one hour before closing, and the site closes on the first Monday of the month, or the next day when that Monday is a holiday.

## Udo: Give the Offshore Island Its Own Day

Udo sits off Jeju’s eastern coast, close enough to see from Seongsan Ilchulbong but far enough to need a separate crossing. Ferries leave from the eastern ports of Seongsan and Jongdal.

The outward crossing is only one part of the plan. Time on Udo and the return ferry also belong in the day. A visit to the eastern coast does not automatically include a passage to the island.

One guided Udo outing in the supplied route guide includes the return ferry and island admission. Its hotel pickup area is Jeju City, while admission to Seongsan and Sangumburi is extra. Those details matter when comparing it with a mainland tour.

For a shorter eastern day, stay on Jeju and combine Seongsan with other nearby stops. For Udo, set aside the day for the crossing and the island instead of trying to fit it between a cave visit and a southern waterfall.

## Manjanggul: A Walk Inside a Lava Tube

![A waterfall pours into a rocky pool surrounded by dense trees, with a lit walkway along the right bank at dusk.](https://forschungsergebnisse.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/1795342758269.webp)

Manjanggul runs for approximately 7.4 kilometers beneath eastern Jeju. Visitors can walk through a public section of about one kilometer, a small part of a much longer underground passage.

Lava once moved through the tube. Near the end of the visitor route stands a 7.6-meter lava column, a large reminder that molten rock did not leave a smooth, empty pipe behind.

A rockfall closed the cave at the end of 2023. It reopened in May 2026 after improvements to the visitor path. An estimated 12,000 to 13,000 bats inhabit the cave, mainly in sections closed to visitors.

Published admission is ₩4,000 for adults and ₩2,000 for children and teenagers. The listed hours are 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., with closure on the first Wednesday of each month. Sources give different last-entry times, 5 p.m. and 5:10 p.m., so arrive before 5 p.m. and confirm the final entry time locally.

## Gujwa and the Eastern Coast

Manjanggul sits in Gujwa, an area suited to an eastern Jeju stage. Pair the cave with coastal stops rather than placing it on the same day as several attractions across the island.

Seongsan is also in the east, but its administrative address belongs to Seogwipo-si. Manjanggul belongs to Jeju-si. Neither label alone tells a visitor how close a place is to central Seogwipo or Jeju City.

Public transport can involve several changes. One described airport journey to Manjanggul uses buses 101, 201, and 711-1 or 711-2, followed by a short walk. That is a workable route, but the connections take planning.

If a ferry to Udo is part of the trip, keep its port and return passage separate from the cave plan. An eastern day can cover volcanic scenery and the coast without also becoming a race to catch a boat.

## Seongeup Folk Village: Houses, Fields, and Stone Walls

At Seongeup Folk Village, thatched roofs and stone walls introduce a different side of Jeju. The materials are simple, and the buildings put the island’s cultural history alongside its volcanic scenery.

The walls extend beyond village lanes. Jeju’s stone field walls are called *batdam*, and they remain part of local farming practice. They show how stone gathered from the land became useful around cultivated fields.

A village stop gives an eastern itinerary a change of pace. At Seongsan, visitors look across a volcanic crater. At Manjanggul, they walk through a lava tube. At Seongeup, the focus shifts to homes and the work of people who live on the island.

Leave time to look at the roofs and the walls rather than treating the village as a brief pause on the way elsewhere. These are features of a living place, not just shapes arranged for a photograph.

## Haenyeo: Jeju’s Women Divers

Jeju’s *haenyeo* gather seafood from the sea without breathing equipment. They dive using knowledge built through practice, including an understanding of the water, the work, and their own limits.

Some divers descend as deep as 10 meters, and some continue working into their 80s. When they return to the surface, they make a distinctive sound as they release their breath. It is one of the recognizable parts of their diving tradition.

The work is part of Jeju’s living maritime culture. It connects families and coastal communities to the sea in a way that a view from the shore cannot fully explain.

The [Jeju Haenyeo Museum](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?menuSn=351&vcontsId=103315) introduces visitors to the divers and their culture. It makes a useful cultural stop alongside an eastern coastal route.

## Jeju Olle: Following the Island on Foot

Jeju Olle is a network of walking routes that connects villages, coastlines, and island landscapes. The first course opened in September 2007, giving visitors another way to travel between places instead of seeing them only from the road.

A 2020 account described 26 trails covering 425 kilometers, including courses on smaller offshore islands. Those are historical figures, not a verified count of the network today.

An Olle walk can fit into a regional day. A stretch near the coast offers a different pace from a drive between Seongsan and Gujwa, while a southern walk can sit alongside time in Seogwipo.

Choose a route for the time available and the distance you want to walk. An offshore course also needs the same care as any other island visit: the walk begins only after the ferry has carried you there.

## Jeongbang Falls: A Waterfall at the Coast

Jeongbang Falls is about 1.5 kilometers southeast of central Seogwipo. The waterfall drops 23 meters and is approximately 8 meters wide, sending water down toward the sea.

Its position makes it a natural southern highlight. After days spent looking at volcanic cones and underground lava formations, the falls offer a different view of the island: fresh water meeting a rocky coast.

Published visitor hours are 9 a.m. to 5:20 p.m. Admission is listed at ₩2,000 per adult and ₩1,000 per child. The short distance from central Seogwipo makes Jeongbang easier to pair with other sights in the city area.

Give the falls room in the schedule. A stop can be close on a map and still feel rushed if the day also includes a long drive, a mountain visit, and several attractions in another part of Jeju.

## Seogwipo and Jungmun: Two Southern Bases

![A camera, map, and notebook sit on a seaside table overlooking a coastal village and flat-topped mountain at sunset.](https://forschungsergebnisse.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/1795342771719.webp)

Seogwipo and Jungmun are both useful for exploring southern Jeju, but they are not the same place. Central Seogwipo suits a day focused on Jeongbang and other nearby sights. Jungmun is a separate destination farther along the southern route.

Accommodation location affects the start and end of each day. A hotel address within the wider Seogwipo administrative area does not necessarily mean a short trip to a central Seogwipo meeting point.

Airport bus route 600 runs through Jungmun toward Seogwipo. Listed travel times are about 50 minutes to Jungmun Tourist Complex and 80 minutes to Seogwipo KAL Hotel. Routes 800 and 800-1 provide other connections into the Seogwipo area.

For a guided outing, match the hotel address to the actual pickup area or meeting point. One southern tour in the route guide starts from meeting points in Jeju City, which is a different arrangement from pickup at a southern hotel.

## Getting the Waterfall Names Right

Jeju has several waterfalls with similar-sounding names. Jeongbang Falls is near central Seogwipo. Cheonjiyeon is another Seogwipo destination. Cheonjeyeon Falls, by contrast, is in Jungmun.

That difference matters when arranging a bus, a driver, or a guided day. Asking for Cheonjeyeon when the intended stop is Cheonjiyeon sends the journey toward a different part of the south.

A route based in central Seogwipo can give Jeongbang the attention it deserves without also treating Jungmun as the same neighborhood. A separate Jungmun stage can include Cheonjeyeon and leave time for the trip between locations.

Write the full waterfall name into the day’s plan. It is a small step, but it prevents a simple mix-up from eating into the hours reserved for seeing the falls.

## Choosing How to Travel Across Jeju

A guided regional tour joins a set of stops with transport and a defined schedule. Some include attraction tickets, while others leave particular admission charges or activities outside the price.

For example, the eastern farm-access outing in the supplied guide includes a vehicle, a guide, and attraction admission. Tangerine picking costs extra. The Udo outing includes the return ferry, but its Seongsan and Sangumburi admissions are separate.

A private taxi, car, or van allows an individually agreed route. Self-driving puts the route and parking in your hands. Rental drivers need the appropriate International Driving Permit, passport, and payment documents, along with the documents required by the rental company.

Public buses suit a trip built around stops and connections. Whichever transport you choose, distinguish a mountain sightseeing stop from a summit hike. A guided southwestern outing with a Hallasan stop is not the same trip as climbing Seongpanak or Gwaneumsa to Baengnokdam.

## Building an Itinerary by Region

Begin with the east. Seongsan Ilchulbong, Gujwa, Manjanggul, and Seongeup offer several combinations of crater, coast, cave, and village. Choose the stops that fit the available hours rather than trying to collect every name in one day.

Give Udo its own excursion. The ferry leaves from Seongsan or Jongdal, and the return journey must fit around the time spent on the island. Seeing Udo from Sunrise Peak is not a substitute for planning the crossing.

Use another day for the south. Central Seogwipo and Jungmun can be joined in a longer route, but their sights should be identified by location. Jeongbang belongs near the city, while Cheonjeyeon belongs in Jungmun.

Set aside a separate day for a Hallasan summit hike. The roughly five-hour ascent leaves little reason to add a distant waterfall or ferry afterward. A southwestern coastal day can stand on its own instead.

## Practical Details That Shape the Day

Weather on Hallasan can differ from conditions at the coast. The mountain reaches 1,950 meters, so a pleasant morning near the sea does not describe the entire route to Baengnokdam.

Transport also changes the shape of a day. The airport bus guide lists about 50 minutes from Jeju International Airport to Jungmun on route 600. A public-bus trip to Manjanggul can require three services and a walk.

Ferries add another step. Udo departures use Seongsan or Jongdal, while the southwest islands of Gapa and Marado have passenger departures from Unjin. These are separate crossings, not automatic additions to a coastal drive.

Prices, hours, access rules, schedules, and ferry arrangements can change, so confirm them shortly before travel. Leave enough space between stops for the journey itself, especially when the day includes a bus connection, a ferry, or a long walk.

## Conclusion: From Sunrise Peak to Southern Falls

Jeju’s scenery makes more sense when its stops are given time. At Seongsan Ilchulbong, a 30- to 40-minute climb leads to a crater above the sea. At Manjanggul, a one-kilometer visitor route reveals a small section of a much longer lava tube.

Hallasan asks for a different commitment. Its summit trails lead toward Baengnokdam after an ascent of about five hours. Udo asks for a ferry. Jeongbang Falls, near central Seogwipo, offers a southern finish with water dropping toward the coast.

Between those landmarks are the island’s people and paths: the haenyeo who gather seafood without breathing equipment, the stone field walls at Seongeup, and Jeju Olle routes connecting villages and coastlines.

Plan the east, the south, and the southwest as separate stages. Keep the summit hike and offshore crossings distinct. The reward is a journey with less time spent chasing the next stop and more time looking at the island in front of you.
